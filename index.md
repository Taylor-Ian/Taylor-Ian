## The 'Why?' for This Website


<div class="custom_indentation" markdown="1"> A lot of why I decided to build myself a wesbite is explained in my [welcome]({% post_url 2026-09-28-Welcome! %}) blog post, so please read that if you want the short and sweet. Shortly after I graduated from Auburn University in August 2024 with a B.S. in Mechanical Engineering, I began working as a power distribution engineer. Doing mundane and monotonous "engineering" work in the stereotypical cubicle setting began to feel soul crushing. Searching and applying for other, more interesting engineering roles was also proving to be fruitless and harder on the ego. I always wanted to do something purposeful, or at least cool and interesting with engineering. If nothing else, I always thought of engineers as folks who built prototypes, ran simulations, designed in CAD, etc. Basically, I didn't think the day to day was actually that dry, and I kind of thought the cubicle setting was just satire for the movies and TV shows. before continuing any further, let me clear the air on a couple things related to my work so I don't give the wrong impression:
</div>

1. There's actually nothing wrong with my work and my problems weren't with my company. It is good and honest work (people need power, it is a necessity not an ammenity these days). 
2. My company had a great work-life balance and all my co-workers and bosses treated me fairly. I am extremely grateful for all those opportunities and the people I worked with.
3. My vision of what engineering was supposed to like was largely my own misconception and lack of understanding or research.


<div class="custom_indentation" markdown="1"> 
Anyways, with that out of the way I will continue on to address the crux of the issues I was having. Since the job search was discouraging and the day to day didn't feel that purposeful, I had to do some soul searching and reconciliation about what my purpose was and what purpose looked like in general. I won't bore anyone with excessive details of that rumination, but I basically came to the following conclusions on the subject of purpose and one's calling:
</div>

1. Work and your career can be a source of purpose, but its okay, perhaps preferred to separate profession from purpose. You should not hate your work, but genuinely loving it and it being your primary source of purpose would just be a huge bonus.

2. In the area which the things you enjoy doing overlap with receiving and spreading spiritual enrichment, is where purpose and calling can be found.

3. Correct time and place are generally separate from purpose and calling. It is much more important that regardless of time and place, you satisfy #2 on this list.

<div class="custom_indentation" markdown="1"> 
If anyone is actually reading all this and paying attention, they're probably thinking, "Okay wait, but---What does this have to do with why you decided to build a website?" Well, let me stop being so dramatic and exestential about the whole thing. While doing all that job searching and not having much success, I figured building an engineering portfolio would help my chances. Then I had all those epiphanies about purpose, and I decided (probably as a coping mechanism) that finding a new job didn't matter as much since it was really never the problem. So the engineering protfolio thing switched from being a portfolio to being this fun creative outlet I could use to organize my life and my projects in a more consolidated and organized way. I would use it as a way to become more employable if possible, but that would cease to be the only purpose. Ultimately I thought it could become this neat scrapbook kind of thing I could look back on and reminisce about how I was spending my time.

## My Engineering Process
*My engineering process is always subject to itself and therefore continuously being refined.*

### Introduction
<div class="custom_indentation" markdown="1"> 
There are probably countless definitions of the engineering process, engineering method, engineering design, or the design process in existence. I learned some of them myself in various classes throughout college. For example, ABET defines the Engineering Design as such:
</div>
> Engineering design is a process of devising a system, component, or process to meet desired needs and specifications within constraints. It is an iterative, creative, decision-making process in which the basic sciences, mathematics, and engineering sciences are applied to convert resources into solutions. Engineering design involves identifying opportunities, developing requirements, performing analysis and synthesis, generating multiple solutions, evaluating solutions against requirements, considering risks, and making trade-offs, for the purpose of obtaining a high-quality solution under the given circumstances. [...]
>— ABET, *Criteria for Accrediting Engineering Programs, 2025–2026*, [abet.org](https://www.abet.org/accreditation/accreditation-criteria/criteria-for-accrediting-engineering-programs-2025-2026/)

<div class="custom_indentation" markdown="1"> 
The way I see it, engineering predates science and the engineering process has been happening informally for millenia. I would guess that before specialization ever took place, the common person was either intentionally or accidentally engineering since the beginning of human kind. Folks knew what needs they had and would brainstorm, protoype, and test until these needs were met. Other times, solutions were stumbled upon by accident and changed the way our ancestors lived. For personal projects I generaly follow my own rudimentary take on the engineering process:
</div>

1. Establish Needs
2. Search For Existing Products
3. Brainstorm Solutions
4. Prototype + Test + Finalize
5. Refine

This generally proves to be sufficient for what I need. Additionally, personal projects can be driven by uneconomical or ludicrous needs. I am not at the whim of any corporation when I build for personal reasons, so the need and solution wouldn't always make sense to the masses. The about section for the GitHub repo of this site summarizes this mentality best:

"These projects were created for no other reason than to express myself through the therapeutic & meditative exercise of intense concentration to achieve an otherwise purposeless goal."
[GitHub Repo](https://github.com/Taylor-Ian/Taylor-Ian/tree/minimal-mistakes)

### 1. Establish Needs
<div class="custom_indentation" markdown="1"> 
Most people would call this stage "Define the Problem," or something of the sort. I generally start with needs because I don't always have a real problem that needs solving, I have needs. As I stated in the introduction, these can appear arbitrary and senseless to most. Other times, they are perfectly legitimate to the masses and reflect something a corporation would also endeavor to do. A need can look like, "I want to build <u> XYZ </u> because that seems cool."
</div>

### 2. Search For Existing Products - Build Your Own Regardless
<div class="custom_indentation" markdown="1"> 
Next you need to avoid designing and building something for no reason by looking around for an existing solution. If you were a consultant, you would give your customer a solid recommendation of what to buy, charge for them for your time and move on. However, this is when I typically decide that I want to build my own solution anyways. The reasons can be expanded capability, cost, or just to learn about the thing I want to build. Once, I built a fully 3d printed submersible water pump for the latter reasoning. 
</div>

### 3. Brainstorm Solutions
<div class="custom_indentation" markdown="1"> 
Before touching CAD and before making any purchases, just think about what could potentially work to meet your need. Do some research on the thoughts that come to mind. Re-visit your needs and ask how accurate you need your solution to be. How robust does it need to be? How long does it have to last? Is it analog or digital, or are there aspects of both? What kind of conditions must it endure? Will you be making many of this design or is it just a one off? Asking these questions early help to eliminate bad designs before you prototype. Do preliminary engineering calculations or simulations whenever needed.
</div>

### 4. Prototype + Test + Finalize

<div class="custom_indentation" markdown="1"> 
Build the simplest possible working example of your solution. Test early and test often. Use the feedback you receive to make changes until something sticks. If you don't care how your design looks or how cleanly it is packaged, you can often stop here and just run with it.
</div>

### 5. Refine

<div class="custom_indentation" markdown="1"> 
Clean up the prototype by making it look good, be more compact, more permanent, or whatever other criteria it may need to fit. If this is something that will be mass produced, that should have been at least partially considered in the brainstorming stage so your design isn't relying on some almost non-existent components. Swap out that Arduino or Raspberry Pi for a custom PCB. Clean up your wiring if it has any, and give it a good housing. Do long term testing and fix longevity issues you find. 
</div>
